Fall 2026 - UTM - CSC 413: Neural Networks and Deep Learning

Course Overview

It is very hard to hand-design programs to solve many real-world problems, e.g. distinguishing images of cats v.s. dogs. Machine learning algorithms allow computers to learn from example data, and produce a program that does the job. Neural networks are a class of machine learning algorithms originally inspired by the brain, but which have recently have seen a lot of success at practical applications. They’re at the heart of production systems at large tech companies for image and video processing, speech-to-text, language understanding, and generative AI.

This course gives an overview of both the foundational ideas and the recent advances in neural net algorithms. Roughly the first 2/3 of the course focuses on supervised learning - training the network to produce a specified behavior when one has lots of labeled examples of that behavior. The last 1/3 focuses on unsupervised learning.

Final Project Guidelines

Introduction

The project in this course is an opportunity to develop deep learning application in an area of your own choosing. It also provides the chance to complete a deep learning project that is much closer to a real-world application area, for example in medicine, finance, robotics, commerce, biology, chemistry, physics (or other sciences), social media, or other fields.

While this project has some structure, you will be required to deal with the ambiguity and significant decision making that make up the life of a deep learning practitioner.

Project Proposal

Logistics

Projects must be done in groups of 3-4. Please form groups on CrowdMark. Exceptions to this rule can be made only in rare cases provided there is good reason to do so. Email the instructors if this applies to you. If you do not know anyone in class feel free to post a message on Piazza. We will also set aside some time during the tutorial for students who are looking for collaborators to find each other and discuss forming a group.

The project proposal is due by November 2, 2026. The proposal describes your problem domain choice, goals, data, and approach. It requires significant thinking, planning, research, and exploratory data analysis. It is to be written in the style of half of an academic paper (4-6 pages) using the NeurIPS LaTeX template. Writing parts of the paper before conducting the research is a way to clarify your ideas in a concrete, visible, and understandable way, so that these ideas can be discussed meaningfully (see Write the Paper First and How to Write a Great Research Paper, though the latter is not ML-focused). You will receive TA feedback on both your ideas/approach and your writing, and you are expected to apply that feedback in your final report.

On the Use of Generative AI

Using generative AI for the project deliverables, the project proposal and the final project report, is prohibited. Using generative AI to debug code is allowed. Instructors are allowed to take measures to detect the use of generative AI in project deliverables, and to apply grading penalties as they deem appropriate. Instructors also have the option to probe and perform further verification of a student’s understanding of submitted project deliverables if they see that the student cannot explain the work that they submitted, either individually or as a group.

Project Requirements

By default, your project must either take a sequence (of variable length) as an input, or produce a sequence as an output, or both. If you have a project proposal that does not involve sequences, please contact the instructors.

Your model should thus involve an RNN or a Transformer component. Students who want to use methods that we have not covered in the course (e.g. diffusion models, neural ODEs) are free to do so, as long as they confirm their methodology with the instructors before they submit this project proposal. There is also flexibility for students to pursue an open research problem. If any groups want to attempt this, they need to discuss this with one of the instructors before the project proposal deadline.

Here are some examples of possible projects:

  • Using an RNN (or transformer) to classify sequences (e.g. whether a restaurant review is positive or negative)
  • Using a generative RNN to produce sequences (e.g. South Park TV scripts)
  • Using a Siamese network to determine whether two StackOverflow questions are duplicates
  • Predict the next item in a sequence (e.g. Stock market)
  • Predict the outcome of a patient based on some sequential factors
  • Predict the dynamics of objects under contact and collision (e.g. robotics and graphics)
  • Generate molecules, or predict properties of molecules

Before choosing a project, consider whether there is data available for you. Since the project deadline is about a month away, consider tailoring your project ideas to what data is available to you.

There are many places to find public datasets for machine learning. Here are a few suggestions, but feel free to search for your own:

  • https://kaggle.com/datasets/
  • https://www.openml.org/search?type=task&sort=runs
  • https://archive.ics.uci.edu/datasets
  • https://data.ontario.ca/ and other government open data initiatives

You are encouraged to use transfer learning and data augmentation ideas in your project.

You can use deep learning packages (e.g. pytorch, huggingface). However, you should be able to explain the steps involved in the forward pass computation of your model.

Project Proposal Rubric

The project proposal document should be 4-6 pages and use the NeurIPS LaTeX template. It is graded out of 40 points.

Section Points Criteria for full credit
Abstract 4 Concise summary that describes your goals, task and model. The abstract length is appropriate (about half a page), and provides enough information about your data, task and approach for a potential reader TA to decide whether they have the expertise to review your work. The abstract is often used to assign reviewers in real conferences, and we may do the same to assign TAs.
Introduction 4 Clear explanation of the “what”, “why”, and “so what” of your project: what is your project about? Why is it important? Why is that meaningful? This section should make your general approach clear, as well as convince the reader that your deep learning approach is reasonable (worth trying out) for this problem.
Background and Related Work 4 Provides TAs and other CSC413 students with enough information to understand the rest of your paper, and describes at least 1-2 prior works related to your project to put it into context. The work chosen should be relevant to your particular domain, not just the general method: if you are using transformers to generate video game music, citing the paper that introduces transformers is not sufficient. Instead, cite papers that work on similar tasks. Likewise, if you are using a type of model not discussed in class, or domain ideas (e.g. music theory) that a CSC413 TA would not be familiar with, briefly describe them and cite a source. Claims are supported by relevant citations, and the models you reference are cited.
Data 4 A clear description of the data and reproducible steps to clean and format it. The description should give the reader a sense of the difficulty of the problem, and help them understand whether your model architecture is appropriate given the nature of your data. Your description should convince the TA that your group thoroughly explored the features, and can identify and communicate key information to the reader.
Model Architecture 4 Rough description of the type(s) of neural network model(s) that you will use, and the relevant components. For full credit, the TAs should be able to understand why you chose this architecture.
Model Architecture Figure 2 A well-thought-out figure that communicates the core idea of your project and architecture immediately. Text is legible and approximately the same size as the main text. Clarity and communicative power are key: an overly complex figure may lack clarity, and an overly simple figure might not communicate much.
Ethical Considerations 2 Thoughtful consideration of ethical issues in data collection, and the impact of using or misusing the model. We are looking for depth of thought beyond what may be “obvious”.
Work Division 4 The division of work is well thought out and fair. There should be enough detail that if a new person were to replace a member of your team, they could understand their responsibilities, estimate their time commitment during each week, and identify conflicts.
Writing: Structure & Organization 4 Sections and subsections are ordered appropriately. Content in each section matches the header. Similar content is grouped under a single section, and section sizes are appropriate. Within each section, information is ordered coherently. Paragraphs have topic sentences and are easy to skim. Figures and tables are labeled with appropriate captions and referenced in the main text. Figures and tables are readable, with font sizes similar to the body. Follows the template’s spacing and formatting guidelines.
Writing: Writing Mechanics 4 Sentences are clear and concise, complete, and not too long or complex. Verbs are descriptive and precise rather than generic. Symbols are annotated with the type of entity they represent. Punctuation is correct, and there are no large amounts of distracting typos and/or grammatical issues.
Writing: Audience Expectations 4 Academic language is used, without slang, impersonal language (“we” is fine, but “I” and “you” are not), or gendered language. Assumed technical knowledge is appropriate for the reader. Acronyms, and terms and concepts unfamiliar to a typical CSC413 student, are defined/explained when first used. Figures that contain text are accessible: images with text are avoided, and captions are descriptive and can be understood by readers with visual impairment.

Advice

Getting Started

One major misconception about academic writing is that papers are written in the same order that they are read. In fact, the introduction and abstract are often some of the last sections that a researcher will write. We suggest that you work on your project proposal in the following order:

  1. Start by exploring the various datasets available and choose one that matches your interests. Since finding and collecting data can be time-consuming, choose your project based on what datasets are available. As you decide on the dataset, perform exploratory analysis and write the Data section of your proposal. (See “Iterative Writing Process” below; do not aim to write full sentences at this point, jot notes are perfectly fine.)
  2. Once you have a clear idea of the data, search for related work that uses that dataset, or a similar dataset. Use this research to write the Background and Related Work section.
  3. Based on your research, propose a model architecture. Write the Model Architecture section and draw your figure. This is also a great time to revise the Related Work section with a description of how your model differs from existing work.
  4. Consider the work that must be done to build this model, and a baseline model to compare with. What work is required? Use this information to write the Work Division section.
  5. Consider the limitations and potential ethical issues arising from your approach. Write the Ethical Considerations section.
  6. Now that you have an idea of what the project will be about, write the Introduction. Although you are now familiar with your project, the introduction is one of the first sections that your reader will see. Clearly explain why your task is important, and what that task really is. The motivation piece is extremely important!
  7. Finally, write your Abstract. You might start by piecing together key sentences from your introduction, data, and model architecture sections, then refine them so that they flow well.

As you write each section, you may wish to go back and revise previous sections. This is a great sign, but be careful that you are also making progress: starting a new section is hard and revising is typically easier. When first writing, start by getting all of the information on the page. Once it is there, think more carefully about structure: what are your paragraphs going to be? Are all your terms defined? Are your sentences clear? What other assumptions are you making about the reader?

Iterative Writing Process

Ideally, your paper should have a clear sentence structure, polished graphics, and a good reading flow.

Counterintuitively, it is faster to create an initial rough draft and iterate over it multiple times than to try to get it right from the beginning. One helpful approach is to first create bullet points, and put all the equations and empty placeholders for tables and figures into the paper. Each bullet point represents a one-sentence summary of a full paragraph. Next, enrich the bullet points with the content that goes into each paragraph. This leaves the worry of finding the best formulation until the end, making text creation much easier since all content is already there.

Similarly, for figures, create a pen & paper / tablet draft and only create a polished figure once the draft conveys the content you want it to convey. Editing a drawing is much faster than editing a diagram in your paper.

Details for Reproducibility

Your paper should have enough detail for reproducibility: another CSC413 student should be able to read your paper and take the same steps you did to achieve similar results (and produce similar figures). Important information includes:

  • How did you format your data? For example, did you use one-hot embeddings or treat a variable as a numeric value? Did you normalize your features?
  • What data did you include/exclude?
  • What is your model architecture?
  • What hyperparameters did you use?
  • What were your other model settings? Why did you choose these?

However, your paper should not contain excessive details that are not required for reproducibility. If someone else could have used a different approach to reproduce your work, that description is likely not useful (e.g. the variable names you used, or the specific piece of code you used to visualize results). The exception is that researchers typically report the tools they used (e.g. PyTorch, pandas, NumPy), both to give credit to the developers and to acknowledge that implementation differences may affect model outcomes.

Captioning and Referencing Figures and Tables

All figures and tables should be captioned, and should additionally be referenced in the main text. For more on writing captions, see How to Write a Figure Caption. Remember that images are less accessible than text: captions are important for people with visual impairments or who otherwise use a screen reader.

Referencing figures and tables does not need to be onerous. For example: “The overall model architecture is illustrated in Figure 1.” or “Table 1 presents the results on the visual quality of the generated videos.” These references typically appear at the beginning or end of a sentence.

In LaTeX papers, figures and tables typically float at the top of the page. Use \begin{figure}[t] and \begin{table}[t] in LaTeX rather than [h]. Your figure or table will not appear exactly where you reference it in the text, and that’s okay.

Citing Related Work

Claims that you make in your paper should be supported by evidence, either produced in your work or by citing external sources. Please use BibTeX or natbib to track your citations; you will need an in-text citation for every item you wish to appear in the references. For more on when and why to cite, see the U of T Library citing guide.

There are two types of in-text citations:

  • Narrative citations (\citet{...}) highlight work that is especially relevant, using the cited work as a noun in your sentence, e.g. “ uses language models to formulate robot-executing plans from natural language instruction.”
  • Parenthetical citations (\cite{...}) provide evidence for a claim without using much space, and are the more common form in ML papers. You can cite multiple works at once, e.g. \cite{yoshikawa2024, wu2023}. It is typical to put a ~ before the citation so LaTeX does not insert a line break, e.g. “Machine learning is useful~”.

Related work should not be discussed in a disparaging manner. It is okay to discuss differences between methods, but do so respectfully.

Final Project

Submission

The Final Project Report is due by December 8, 2026. The report is written in the style of an academic paper. It has many of the same sections as your proposal, but also describes your final model and an assessment of its performance. Since you will receive detailed writing feedback on your project proposal, you are expected to apply that feedback when writing your final report.

Please submit a file called github.txt containing a link to the github repository. If your repository will be private, please email the instructors by December 1, 2026 so that TAs and instructors can be added, even if you use tokens.

Repository Content

The repository should contain:

  1. The code you used to pre-process the data, but not the data itself. It is generally a bad idea to include data in your github repository, since git is great for lots of small files, but a poor choice for sharing large files. Moreover, most groups are using data collected by other people. While you should share the source of your data, you should generally not share a copy of the data.

  2. The code you used to train your model. You may opt to share model weights, or not.

  3. A README file with the following component:

  • Introduction that states the deep learning model that you are building
  • Model:
    • A figure/diagram of the model architecture that demonstrates understanding of the steps involved in computing the forward pass
    • Count the number of parameters in the model, and a description of where the parameters come from
    • Examples of how the model performs on two actual examples from the test set: one successful and one unsuccessful
  • Data:
    • Describe the source of your data
    • Provide summary statistics of your data to help interpret your results (similar to in the proposal)
    • Describe how you transformed the data (e.g. any data augmentation techniques)
    • If appropriate to your project, describe how the train/validation/test set was split. (Note that splitting the training/validation/test set is not always straightforward!)
  • Training:
    • The training curve of your final model
    • A description how you tuned hyper-parameters
  • Results:
    • Describe the quantitative measure that you are using to evaluate your result
    • Describe the quantitative and qualitative results
    • A justification that your implemented method performed reasonably, given the difficulty of the problem—or a hypothesis for why it doesn’t (this is extremely important)
  • Ethical Consideration:
    • Description of a use of the system that could give rise to ethical issues. Are there limitations of your model? Your training data?
  • Authors
    • A description of how the work was split—i.e. who did what in this project.

Paper and Section Length

Your paper should be exactly 8 pages and use the NeurIPS LaTeX template. You may use additional pages only for references.

The abstract and introduction should take about 1.5 pages. The background and related work section should be no more than 1 page. The data section should be about 1-1.5 pages. The model architecture should also be about 1 page, including the figure. The results and discussion should take about 2-3 pages. The limitations and ethical considerations are about half a page, and the conclusion should be at most half a page.

Deviation from this is certainly possible: for example, if your data is particularly complex, it may require more explanation than the 1-1.5 page guideline. You shouldn’t see these guidelines as rules: good writing practices may mean that some sections are longer or shorter, and you may receive writing feedback in that regard. However, a large deviation without good reason is usually a sign that a part of the report is weak or lacks sufficient detail for reproduction.

Publishing Your Work

Beyond the course, strong final reports are encouraged to go one step further: the Department of Mathematical and Computational Sciences runs a student research journal, the MCS Journal, hosted on the University of Toronto’s journal platform. By submitting your project you will have your written work peer-reviewed and published. You will also gain experience of the whole process of academic research. As your final report is already formatted as an academic paper and received some feedback from your TAs, it makes a great starting point. This is completely optional and not related to the course assessment. However, it would be a great way for you to take your work further.
